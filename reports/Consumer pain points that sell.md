# Lead with gifting, using comfort products

Gifting is the pain point the evidence supports most. Gift buying drives the November and December window, and the NRF's 2025 survey puts **about 70% of planned holiday spend on gifts (\$627.93 of \$890.49 per person)** ([NRF](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/consumers-to-spend-second-highest-amount-on-record-according-to-nrf-holiday-survey)). Gift-oriented pages also carry the highest conversion benchmarks of the four categories, at **4.0% to 5.0%** against **1.0% to 1.4%** for home decor, though those benchmarks come from search summaries and vendor blogs. Comfort and self-care show strong self-reported demand, and candles peak in Q4, but no source measures a sales lift from comfort purchases. Cold-snap spikes are real in individual cases but anecdotal and dated. Everyday carry has the weakest case: the lowest conversion benchmark (**0.8%**), market estimates that differ about tenfold, and no gift-share data. For Sullivan Ridge, the practical reading is to sell gifting as the frame and use the blankets, candles and bath products already in the catalog as the gifts. The holiday window started this month, so the owner should act this week and measure results rather than wait for better benchmarks.

## Gift demand concentrates the spending that matters

Online holiday spend is large but growing more slowly. Adobe measured **\$257.8B** in US online holiday spend for November 1 to December 31, 2025, up **6.8%** from 2024 and below the **8.7%** growth of 2024 ([The Star, summarizing Adobe](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/01/07/us-online-holiday-spending-hits-record-levels-despite-slower-growth-adobe-says)). The Adobe release itself returned a 403 and was not read directly. Adobe's category data shows no home-goods or gift line, so the gift share of online sales remains unmeasured in these sources. The 2025 outcome also conflicts across secondary coverage: **4.1%** growth in one account ([Demandsage](https://www.demandsage.com/?p=24837)) and **3.1%** growth to \$972B in another ([Walnut Invest](https://walnutinvest.com/stats/holiday-spending-statistics)). Treat both as unverified.

Gift intent is the largest single share of holiday budgets. The NRF and Prosper Insights survey (8,247 adults, October 1 to 7, 2025) found planned spend of **\$890.49 per person, down 1.3%** from the prior year, with \$627.93 for gifts ([NRF](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/consumers-to-spend-second-highest-amount-on-record-according-to-nrf-holiday-survey)). The caution comes from Deloitte's stated-intent survey, which the notes confirm from the fetched release: planned spend is **\$1,595 per person, down 10%**, with retail goods expected to fall **14%** and experiences **6%** ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/about/press-room/deloitte-holiday-spending-declines-amid-economic-uncertainty.html)). Physical goods, the category a gift store sells, are where shoppers cut first. Deloitte also reports that **24%** of shoppers expect their budget to run out by the end of October, and half are spreading purchases across the season.

Timing is the second reason to act now. The Prosper table implies that about **42%** of 2025 shoppers started before November (8.4% before September, 7.9% in September and 25.6% in October), and the NRF release cites **43%** ([Chain Store Age](https://chainstoreage.com/consumers-start-holiday-shopping-november-spend-same-amount-last-year)). The same report text says "well over half" start in October or earlier, which conflicts with its own table. This report uses a range of 40% to 45%. Gift content must be live before most buying starts, and that point has already passed for the early share of shoppers. The gap that would settle the question is a verified gift share of e-commerce or a Nov-Dec share for home-goods gift sales. Statista and eMarketer were not reached, so that figure is missing.

## Gift-intent pages convert best, but every benchmark is soft

The conversion benchmarks point the same way as the demand data, but they are weak evidence on their own. The table below lists the figures the notes retained, with their sourcing.

| Category | Benchmark conversion | Sourcing |
|---|---|---|
| Gifts under \$75 / gifts and specialty | 4.0% to 5.0% | [Bloom Analytics](https://www.bloomanalytics.io/blog/shopify-conversion-rate-optimization), [Thunderbit](https://thunderbit.com/blog/shopify-conversion-rate-statistics); search summaries |
| Home decor and furniture | 1.0% to 1.9% (most cited 1.0% to 1.4%) | [Skailama](https://skailama.com/blog/ecommerce-conversion-rate-by-industry), [EightX](https://eightx.co/blog/average-conversion-rate-by-ecommerce-vertical-2026), [Blend Commerce](https://blendcommerce.com/blogs/shopify/ecommerce-conversion-rate-benchmarks-2026); not fetched |
| Luggage, bags, travel accessories | 0.8% (Salesforce via Wisepim); 1.8% in an earlier snapshot | [Wisepim](https://wisepim.com/ecommerce-industry-insights/luggage-bags), fetched for 0.8% |
| Candles, bath, blankets | None found | No primary source |
| All e-commerce | About 2.5% to 2.7% | [Statsig](https://statsig.com/perspectives/ecommerce-conversion-rate-industry), Wisepim |

The gift benchmark runs about three times the home-decor midpoint, and the notes say the direction is more reliable than the multiple. Methods differ: some benchmarks divide transactions by sessions and others by visitors, and some cover only purchase-intent traffic ([Fudge.ai](https://www.fudge.ai/blog/shopify-conversion-rate-benchmarks/)). No source reports average order value for gift versus everyday purchases, and none measures conversion on gift-guide pages. Repeat purchase is the one primary-source benchmark: Klaviyo's glossary, which was opened directly, calls a repeat rate of **20% to 30%** "good" and notes that affordable or perishable goods repeat more ([Klaviyo](https://klaviyo.com/glossary/what-is-a-good-repeat-purchase-rate)). Candles and bath consumables plausibly sit at the high end, but no candle-specific figure supports that. On returns, NRF and Happy Returns forecast a **19.3%** online return rate for 2025 across all categories ([NRF](https://cdn.nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/consumers-expected-to-return-nearly-850-billion-in-merchandise-in-2025)). Luggage returns are reported near **12.9%**, but that figure has no stated date ([Wisepim](https://wisepim.com/ecommerce-industry-insights/luggage-bags)).

## Comfort sells as stress relief, but no source measures a lift

Self-reported demand for comfort is strong. In a LendingTree survey fielded June 2025 (n=2,000), **54%** of US adults consider retail therapy a form of self-care, **47%** shop to improve their mood, and **38%** say financial stress has led them to spend more ([LendingTree](https://www.lendingtree.com/credit-cards/study/emotional-shopping/)). NCSolutions (April 2025, n=1,036) found **62%** count small treats as part of their self-care routine ([NCSolutions](https://ncsolutions.com/?p=62448)). Afterpay's holiday report says **71%** plan to buy self-care items this season ([Cash App Afterpay](https://cash.app/press/afterpay-holiday-report-2025)), but the method was not checked and the sponsor is a lender. Circana's July 2025 read is that cost-conscious consumers prioritize essentials and products that bring joy at home ([Mammoth Times, Circana](https://business.mammothtimes.com/mammothtimes/article/gnwcq-2025-7-10-consumers-are-prioritizing-the-essentials-as-well-as-products-that-bring-joy-to-cost-conscious-at-home-living-reports-circana)). That is an analyst observation, not a measured sales effect. The survey sources also differ in wording, so their percentages should not be merged.

Candles show the clearest seasonal peak, and it overlaps almost completely with gifting. The National Candle Association reports that **about 35%** of candle sales occur in the holiday season ([NCA](https://candles.org/facts-figures/)), but that figure cites a 2013 Mintel estimate. Vendor guidance is higher, with one candle maker putting holiday at **38% to 40%** of annual sales and about half of that in Q4 ([Candle Science](https://www.candlescience.com/the-candle-maker-s-guide-to-a-successful-fall-and-holiday-season/)). Those vendors have a promotional interest. Market size is equally unsettled: the US candle market estimates for 2025 range from **\$1.84B** ([Market Research Future](https://www.marketresearchfuture.com/de/reports/us-candles-market-18177)) to **\$3.81B** ([Market Data Forecast](https://www.marketdataforecast.com/market-reports/united-states-candle-market)), and IBISWorld's manufacturing series shows a **4.5%** decline for 2025 ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/industry-statistics/market-size/candle-manufacturing-united-states/)), which is a snippet read after a 405 error on the direct fetch.

Cold weather is the least measured of the four. Global throw-blanket estimates for 2025 range from **\$2.68B** to **\$7.48B**, and no source gives a US-only figure for blankets or knitwear. The cold-snap evidence is anecdotal. Faribault Mill reported bed-blanket sales up about **75%** during a holiday cold snap ([Star Tribune](https://www.startribune.com/who-won-last-weeks-arctic-blast-minnesotas-parka-and-blanket-makers/600239370)), a snippet-level figure. A 2022 UK report showed fleece-blanket sales up **386%** ([Retail Gazette](https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2022/12/john-lewis-next-winter-sales/)), which is a different country and year. An NPD analyst told NBC in 2019 that a cold snap arriving after post-holiday markdowns had already started blunted its effect ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/freezing-temps-give-stores-january-thaw-flna1C8119013)). That timing point matters more for this store than any spike size. No source links first-freeze dates to retail sales, and the notes exclude several unsourced spike claims of 300%, 38% and 530%. Whether blankets are bought as gifts is also unmeasured. NRF data shows 95% of adults buy for family, but there is no product-level split.

## Everyday carry has the thinnest sales case

Everyday carry has the least support of the four categories. The market size depends on the publisher: US leather goods and luggage is put at **\$52.2B** in 2024 ([PS Market Research](https://www.psmarketresearch.com/market-analysis/us-leather-good-luggage-market-report)), while IBISWorld puts US leather goods and luggage *manufacturing* at **\$3.6B** ([IBISWorld](https://img3.ibisworld.com/united-states/market-size/leather-good-luggage-manufacturing/374)). No source isolates the US travel-accessory slice. The conversion evidence is also the weakest. The fetched Wisepim page puts luggage, bags and travel accessories at **0.8%**, below the **2.68%** e-commerce average. Shopify's Q1 2024 snapshot reports **0.9%** for handbags and luggage, but that figure is a search snippet ([Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/blog/cro-statistics)).

Seasonality is the second weakness. Wisepim describes demand peaking in summer and in November and December, but gives no values and names no source ([Wisepim](https://wisepim.com/ecommerce-industry-insights/luggage-bags)). Google's holiday guide says travel accessories have their best deals in November ([Google](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/shopping/holiday-shopping-when-to-buy-guide/)), which supports a gift-window push but not a size. Underlying travel demand is flat: the US Travel Association projects business travel growth of **0.7%** in 2026 ([Business Travel Executive](https://www.businesstravelexecutive.com/news/usta-projects-slight-growth-in-business-travel-spending-in-2026/)), and Phocuswright reports that trip counts held steady in 2025 while spending declined ([Phocuswright](https://www.phocuswright.com/Travel-Research/Consumer-Trends/US-Consumer-Travel-Report-2025)). On gifts, clothing and accessories ranked as the most-wanted gift category in NRF's 2024 survey (**54%**, via [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/top-gift-categories-of-2024-holiday-season)), but no source isolates travel or commute gear. The two empty travel and commute collections should not get inventory until a gift-positioned test shows demand.

## What the owner should do next

Start with gift merchandising this week. Build collections by recipient, such as a partner, a relative who has everything, or a traveler, because recipient difficulty is the best-supported pain point. A Rakuten survey with an unclear date found spouses or partners to be the hardest recipients to shop for (**20%**), and a Mixbook study reported that **47%** find gift shopping more stressful than arguing with family ([Retail Merchandiser](https://retail-merchandiser.com/news/rakuten-shopping-secrets-survey-results/); [Mixbook](https://app.mixbook.com/inspiration/gifting-anxiety-report), a seller with a commercial interest). Gift wrap and gift-message options have no supporting data in the notes, so test them rather than assume them. Repurpose the empty travel collection as a traveler gift page, which costs little and tests the gift frame directly.

Measure the store's own baseline before trusting any benchmark. Pull conversion and average order value by collection and landing page for the last several weeks, and set a gift-collection target. The 4% to 5% gift benchmark and the 1% to 1.4% home-decor benchmark are directional only, and no source gives a gift average order value to compare against. Track which products appear in gift orders, because the notes say it is unknown whether blankets and candles are bought as gifts. For cold weather, log daily sales next to the local first-freeze date from NOAA. No source in these notes fills that gap, and it would show whether cold snaps lift this store's sales and whether the lift holds in the gift window. Before any of these figures reach a customer, a partner or a lender, verify them. The notes conflict on the early-shopping share (42% against "well over half"), the 2025 holiday growth (4.1% against 3.1%), and the status of the Deloitte 59% gift-stress figure, which is a Philadelphia-area number and not national. Do not quote the WalletHub 42% gift-ideas figure, which has no URL, or the 85% tariff-price figure, which is unverified.

## Conclusion

The four pain points are less separate than they look. Gift buying, cold-weather warmth and comfort goods all sell in the same weeks and draw on the same product set, so the real choice is the frame, not the product. Gifting holds up across demand concentration, timing and gift-intent conversion, while the comfort and carry categories rest mostly on self-report, sponsored surveys or vendor estimates. The largest open question for this store is whether blankets, candles and bath sets sell as gifts at all. These notes cannot answer it, and the store's own November and December orders will answer it faster than any benchmark in them.

The window is short. Holiday spending in the Adobe measure closes at the end of December, so waiting for better research costs part of the season. Launching recipient-based gift pages now, with a baseline and a cold-weather log, is a cheap test that yields the data this report lacks by January.
